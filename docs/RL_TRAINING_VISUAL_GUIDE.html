Start with the distinction

Challenge ≠ task ≠ policy

Challenge

The Sprint rules: travel down a flat 2 m lane as quickly as possible without falling. The Arena tests a range of commanded speeds and scores the best successful one.

Task / environment

The training recipe: robot model, simulated physics, observations, rewards, randomization, and PPO settings. This is the challenge-specific sprint_2m task.

Policy

The learned controller. A trained checkpoint is PyTorch data (model_N.pt); export packages the actor and observation normalizer as an ONNX policy for the Arena.

Robot anatomy: what are the motors doing?

The real robot is a small articulated biped. Motors at the hip, knee and ankle joints position the legs; neck/head joints orient and stabilize the head. The policy does not directly command motor voltage or torque. It requests joint positions, and the servo/actuator dynamics determine how quickly and strongly each joint follows.

The exposed dark rectangular modules are actuator housings. Each actuator has finite torque, friction, inertia, delay and speed limits.

The Sprint actor gets no camera or LiDAR image input. Although the product includes a camera, LiDAR and two IMUs, this walking policy uses body angular velocity, gravity direction, joint state/history and command values. Do not infer that every physical sensor is used by this specific policy.

What does “15 motors” versus “14 policy outputs” mean?

The press kit describes the physical product as having 15 motors. This Sprint task’s documented actor contract is 14 commanded joint targets: the two 5-joint legs plus 4 head/neck joints. A product-wide actuator count and a task-specific action vector are different counts. Keep the 61-observation/14-action interface unchanged for this challenge; never add or remove a channel based only on the product headline count.

How PPO learns

The training loop, one cycle at a time

PPO does not get a script saying “move the left foot now.” It tries actions, sees their consequences, and updates a neural network so useful actions become more likely. A separate value network estimates how good a situation is, helping PPO judge whether an outcome was better or worse than expected.

Observe: the actor receives 61 values—gyro, gravity direction, 14 relative joint angles, 14 joint velocities, previous actions, and 13 command values.
Act: a neural network maps those numbers to 14 joint-position targets. The Arena and training run the control loop at 50 Hz (one decision every 20 ms).
Simulate: MuJoCo advances the robot and contacts. One control period consists of four 5 ms physics steps; an XL330/BAM actuator model turns target error into limited, delayed motor torque.
Score the transition: speed tracking and gait rewards add incentives; falls terminate an episode. The training critic uses extra privileged observations (77 values) unavailable to the actor.
Update and repeat: PPO uses the rollout to adjust its actor and critic. It clips policy changes to reduce destructive updates, and an entropy term encourages continued exploration.
Save checkpoints: after periodic training intervals, a snapshot such as model_1500.pt is saved. Evaluate all useful snapshots: the last one can be worse than an earlier one.

Scale of this run: 4,096 parallel simulated environments × 24 control steps per environment per PPO iteration gives about 98,304 transitions in each rollout. At 3,000 iterations, that is about 295 million environment-step samples, before accounting for PPO’s repeated optimization over the rollout.

The dynamics that shape the gait

What the simulated duck is physically dealing with

Course and termination

  • Flat lane, 2.0 m long and 0.8 m wide, with 0.15 m walls.
  • Starts in the STAND pose; joints are perturbed by ±0.01 rad and yaw by ±2°.
  • 0.5 s settling period is untimed. The policy is already controlling the duck.
  • A sustained trunk tilt beyond 60° for 0.3 s is a fall; the timeout is 50 s.

Control and contact

  • Physics step: 5 ms. Policy/servo control interval: 4 physics steps = 20 ms = 50 Hz.
  • Fourteen active joints: five per leg plus four neck/head joints.
  • Joint targets pass through the BAM XL330 actuator model, not direct torque control.
  • Feet push against the ground; alternating support and swing must create forward impulse without tipping the trunk.

Training disturbances

  • Velocity pushes up to ±0.3 m/s every 3–6 s (training only).
  • Foot friction and body/head center of mass are randomized.
  • IMU mounting orientation, encoder bias, mass/inertia, and armature are randomized.
  • Small observation noise and a one-control-step joint-velocity lag make inputs less idealized.

Why deployment differs slightly

  • The Arena uses nominal physics/randomization values and no training pushes or observation noise.
  • It retains the BAM actuator model, training-matched command delay, and one-step velocity lag.
  • A small training/evaluation mismatch can change a balance-sensitive gait; use official results to confirm a local win.

Why the observation normalizer must be exported too

During training, observations are normalized using running statistics. The policy expects normalized inputs. The project exporter bakes that normalization into the ONNX graph; exporting only the actor weights would feed the wrong scale at runtime and can change behavior even when the network weights are unchanged. Use uv run publish or the supported export path; do not hand-convert model_N.pt.

A learning plan

Experiments that teach you something

Run one controlled experiment at a time. Before each run, write a prediction. Keep the Arena sweep and observation/action layout intact; compare the best 5/5 checkpoint from every run.

1 · Repeat baseline with a new seed

change: seed 1 → seed 2

Question: how much does training randomness alone move the result? If outcomes vary widely, reward tweaks need repeat runs to distinguish signal from luck.

2 · Ask for more speed tracking

forward_reward: 2.0 → 3.0

Hypothesis: stronger speed tracking may move successful commands upward. Watch whether 1.0–1.2 m/s finishes improve or fall rate rises.

3 · Test balance emphasis separately

upright_reward: 2.0 → 3.0

Hypothesis: stronger upright reward may preserve fast finishes, but could make the gait overly cautious. Do not combine with experiment 2 on the first try.

4 · Aim training at fast commands

sample command speeds non-uniformly; keep 0.20–1.20

Question: does sampling high speeds more often make the top-speed policy less brittle? This requires a deliberate command-sampling change in the environment, not narrowing the Arena’s tested range.

Keep an experiment notebook: hypothesis → one edit → test/smoke run → full training → score checkpoints → official entry only after choosing a winner. Save every run to its own checkpoint repo so experiments remain comparable and reproducible.

Example: what exactly is a reward change testing?

The current forward_reward = 2.0 sets the weight of the velocity-tracking reward. Raising it does not directly force the duck to move faster; it increases the training incentive to match the sampled command. The policy can still discover that a command is too risky and fall or move cautiously. The evaluator’s speed sweep is the measurement that decides whether the change helped.

Public vs private, overwrite behavior, and what gets uploaded
  • --no-private --enter: create/update a public policy and submit it to the Arena.
  • For a private Hub policy only, omit both flags. A private model cannot enter the Arena.
  • The publish tool exports the checkpoint as policy.onnx, validates its 61→14 shape and smoke output, and uploads policy metadata.
  • If the target repo already contains policy.onnx, publish stops unless you deliberately add --force. That replaces the existing policy; it does not create a second ONNX file.
  • Use a separate repo per experiment when possible. This preserves prior policies and makes comparison easier.
Understanding rank and the scoring objective

The Sprint sweeps commands 0.20–1.20 m/s in 0.05 m/s increments. Seed 1 screens each speed; the three fastest candidates are re-run on seeds 1–5. Finishes and time determine the mean-finish-time score; failures/timeouts count against the result. The leaderboard ranks score, then seeds finished, then submission time. A high speed that only finishes 1/5 is not a better policy than a slightly slower 5/5 finisher.

If something goes wrong

Common snags and safe next steps

“Model/repo not found” while downloading

Use the checkpoint repo from the training log’s [ckpt] checkpoints -> line. The task ID Mjlab-Sprint2m-MicroDuck is not a Hub repo. The job ID is only for the Job page and inspection.

“already carries policy.onnx” when publishing

The target repo already has a policy. Prefer publishing to a new repo name. If overwriting is intentional, add --force; check repo name and chosen checkpoint before doing so.

The cloud job completed but there is no local logs folder

Cloud job checkpoints live in its Hub checkpoint repo, not automatically in your local workspace. Download that repo with uv run hf download <checkpoint-repo-id> --local-dir logs/rsl_rl.

The best checkpoint is earlier than the last one

Normal. PPO updates are noisy and checkpoint performance can regress. Score the saved checkpoints rather than equating “latest” with “best.”

All local Arena tests pass, but the official score differs

Local machine architecture and simulation details can move timings. Treat local runs as a screening tool, compare policies under the same local evaluator, and use the official Arena entry as final confirmation.

Terminal output shows a token or secret

Never paste access tokens, environment secrets or API keys into chat or a public log. Share job IDs and repository IDs only; redact secret values before sharing diagnostic output.
